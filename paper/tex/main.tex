\title{Capture, Not Retrieve:\\ Behavioural Provenance for Agentic AI}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Munyaradzi Zhou}
\IEEEauthorblockA{University of Trento\\ Trento, Italy\\ munyaradzizhou11@gmail.com}
\and
\IEEEauthorblockN{Sandro Fiore}
\IEEEauthorblockA{University of Trento\\ Trento, Italy\\ sandro.fiore@unitn.it}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Provenance tooling can record what an agentic AI system did; it cannot record why. We argue
that the gap is structural. A decision applies a rule to values it computed, and from outside
the step neither is identifiable: no callback, span or post-hoc query can retrieve what was
never captured. We make the argument measurable with a reflexive multi-agent demonstrator
(four operational agents emitting typed W3C PROV about themselves, two meta agents governing
that output), a deliberately generous callback-level baseline, a line tracer inside the step,
and LangChain's own callback handler on a LangGraph port. Over 24 real yProv4ML training runs,
the baseline reconstructs every activity, lineage relation and verdict, yet of the 432 values
the agents' decision rules read it saw 24, each a field of the input record. Our capture
records each decision as a named, versioned rule applied to its inputs, so every decision
replays from its own record and an edited verdict does not. We publish the \texttt{yp:}
vocabulary as an OWL ontology with SHACL shapes, and bound the cost: building the record is
equivalent to no provenance within 1\,ms, while writing it adds about a millisecond per
record and 16\% CPU energy per session.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
provenance, W3C PROV, agentic AI, explainability, multi-agent systems, SHACL
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

\noindent Scientific computing infrastructure is acquiring autonomous agents: they summarise runs, judge results and decide what to archive, increasingly without a human in the loop. The provenance community has a mature answer to \emph{what} such a system did. W3C PROV~\cite{provdm} describes activities, entities and the lineage between them, and capturing that lineage automatically is routine. It has almost no answer to \emph{why}. When a storage agent declines to archive a record, an operator, an auditor and, under the record-keeping and explanation duties of the EU AI Act (Articles 12 and 86)~\cite{euaiact}, a regulator need the reason, not the outcome.

\noindent We argue that this gap is \textbf{structural, not a matter of tooling}, and state it precisely. A decision step receives inputs $x$, computes intermediate values $z = g(x)$, and returns a verdict $v = r(x, z)$. We call $z$ the \emph{computed facts} and $r$ the \emph{decision rule}. A \emph{boundary observer} (a callback that fires after the step returns, a span that wraps it, a query that reads the store afterwards) sees only what crosses the step's interface: $x$ where it is passed, $v$, and whatever the step chooses to emit.

\noindent \textbf{Proposition 1.} From a boundary observer's record, $z$ is recoverable only by re-running $g$, which the record does not contain, and $r$ is not identifiable: any rule $r'$ with $r'(x, g(x)) = v$ explains the observation equally well, and a two-valued verdict tells the observer one bit about $r$ per decision.

\noindent Retrieving the reason is therefore an identification problem without a unique answer, and only the step itself holds both $z$ and the branch of $r$ it took. An observer \emph{inside} the step, such as a line tracer, sees both, but that is capture by other means, not retrieval; Section~\ref{sec:5.2} measures what it recovers and costs. We call this \textbf{capture, not retrieve}, and make three contributions. \textbf{C1: the argument, measured.} Against a deliberately generous callback-level baseline over the same run, which recovers every activity, relation and verdict, we show that the facts an agent \emph{computed} in order to decide, and the rule that combined them, are unrecoverable from outside the step. \textbf{C2: a reflexive multi-agent architecture.} Four operational agents emit typed PROV about themselves; two meta agents govern that output, to a depth bounded by design. Governance is where capture pays first: a compliance agent can check that every decision is justified and replayable only because the record says why, and over the baseline's record it has nothing to check. \textbf{C3: the \texttt{yp:\allowbreak{}} vocabulary}, published as an OWL ontology with SHACL shapes, and an API whose one hand-written call records a decision that replays from its own record. We bound the cost, in time and in energy.

\section{Background and State of the Art}\label{sec:2}

\subsection{Provenance models and capture systems}\label{sec:2.1}

\noindent W3C PROV~\cite{provdm,provo} models provenance as entities, activities and agents linked by a small set of relations; it is deliberately domain-neutral, and says nothing about rationale. Capture systems differ in where they observe. \emph{Script and workflow} tools (noWorkflow~\cite{noworkflow} for Python scripts, YesWorkflow~\cite{yesworkflow} for annotated workflows) recover lineage from code and execution. \emph{System-level} collectors such as SPADE~\cite{spade} and CamFlow~\cite{camflow} capture whole-system provenance from the operating system, CamFlow emitting W3C PROV-JSON directly. \emph{Machine-learning} trackers record runs, parameters and artifacts: MLflow~\cite{mlflow} with its own schema, and the yProv family (yProv4ML~\cite{yprov4ml} for training runs, yProv4WFs for workflows) in W3C PROV. Surveys of the field~\cite{freire2008,herschel2017} classify provenance by \emph{what} it records and \emph{how} it is captured; across all of them, what is recorded is an observable event. Plans enter PROV through P-Plan~\cite{pplan}, which links an execution to the plan that guided it, but a plan is a prescription made before the run, not a reason computed during it. Our demonstrator consumes real yProv4ML records, and every system above could, in principle, observe the agents we build. None could observe their reasoning.

\subsection{Agents, and the observability built for them}\label{sec:2.2}

\noindent Agent frameworks interleave reasoning and tool use~\cite{react} and compose agents into multi-agent systems~\cite{autogen,langgraph}. Their runs are observed by a fast-maturing layer: OpenTelemetry's semantic conventions for generative AI~\cite{otelgenai}, and tracing platforms such as OpenLLMetry~\cite{openllmetry}, Langfuse~\cite{langfuse} and LangSmith~\cite{langsmith}, which record prompts, completions, tool calls, latencies and token counts. This layer is built for debugging and cost attribution. It does not model W3C PROV, and the only rationale it can hold is text a model happened to emit. PROV-AGENT~\cite{provagent} is the closest prior work: it extends PROV to agentic workflows and makes agents, their tool calls and their model interactions first-class provenance subjects, captured automatically through the Model Context Protocol: a richer \emph{what}, with no term for the rule behind a decision.

\subsection{Rationale, explanation and accountability}\label{sec:2.3}

\noindent Decision provenance~\cite{singh2019} argues that accountability needs data flow traced across the systems that feed and act on an automated decision; it frames the need, and our record is one way to meet it inside the decision itself. Explanation has been modelled as a resource in its own right, notably by the Explanation Ontology~\cite{chari2020}, and agent research has explained belief-desire-intention agents by the beliefs, goals and values behind an action~\cite{winikoff2022}. Both describe what an explanation should contain; neither records it at the moment of decision. Interpretability research asks how a model reaches outputs~\cite{doshivelez2017}, and documentation practice, such as model cards~\cite{modelcards} and datasheets~\cite{datasheets}, records how artifacts were made. Two results bear directly on agents. A model's stated chain of thought is not necessarily faithful to the process that produced its answer~\cite{turpin2023,lanham2023}, so text a model emits is weak evidence of the rule an agent applied. And proposals for the oversight of deployed agents call for exactly the per-action visibility (identifiers, activity logs, the basis of decisions) that a provenance record could carry~\cite{chan2024}. Energy is the third concern of the field: reporting it for ML workloads is now expected~\cite{strubell2019,henderson2020}, usually through sampling tools such as CodeCarbon~\cite{codecarbon}, whose limits for short steps we revisit in Section~\ref{sec:5.3}.

\noindent \textbf{Our position is to ride the automatic layer rather than compete with it}: consume what it collects, and add the one thing it structurally cannot.

\section{Vocabulary and Instrumentation}\label{sec:3}

\noindent \textbf{The \texttt{yp:\allowbreak{}} vocabulary} is published as an OWL ontology (88 terms) with SHACL~\cite{shacl} shapes (18 named constraints). It emits only terms the system uses, checked in both directions by a test, and uses PROV's own term wherever one exists: all lineage relations are PROV's. It adds two things PROV lacks: a typed decision lifecycle (Receive, Plan, Act, Observe, \textbf{Decide}, Respond, as subclasses of \texttt{prov:\allowbreak{}Activity}) and \texttt{yp:\allowbreak{}justification}, with \texttt{rdfs:\allowbreak{}domain yp:\allowbreak{}DecisionActivity}, beside \texttt{yp:\allowbreak{}verdict}, \texttt{yp:\allowbreak{}escalation\_\allowbreak{}reason}, and the \texttt{yp:\allowbreak{}rule} and \texttt{yp:\allowbreak{}rule\_\allowbreak{}inputs} that make a decision replayable. The shapes declare the standard: every decision is justified and concluded; every step is typed, dated, ended and attributed; every agent decides something; every \texttt{yp:\allowbreak{}pid} carries a \texttt{sha256:\allowbreak{}} hash. An agent's own runtime check is tested against the shapes rather than trusted to match them, which has found four disagreements, each fixed.

\noindent When a model chooses the verdict, the step's rule is the model call and the code that parses its answer: the record names the prompt by identity and hash (Section~\ref{sec:5.4}) and the parsed fields as inputs, replay checks the parse, and the call itself is repeatable from its record rather than reproducible. Every verdict evaluated here is reached in code; a model-chosen verdict is where this design reaches least far.

\noindent \textbf{Two layers.} \emph{Layer 1, what happened} (activities, timings, model calls, dataflow) follows mechanically from what a framework callback receives, and the ecosystem of Section~\ref{sec:2.2} already collects it. \emph{Layer 2, why} (the verdict and its justification) cannot be automated. The library automates the first and leaves one call hand-written:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
with tracker.activity("ToolCallActivity", "write the summary") as prov:
    prov.consumes(task)
    summary = llm.complete(ACT_SYSTEM, facts)
    prov.produces("ResponseEntity", summary.text, pid=pointer, derived_from=task)
    prov.note(**summary.provenance())        # model, prompt hash, tokens

tracker.decide(verdict="REVISE",             # the hand-written line
    justification="Figures 4096 and 512 appear in the summary but not in the graph.",
    rule=SUMMARY_RULE,                       # cax.summary/v1: a pure function
    evidence={"ungrounded_figures": 2, "truncated": False})
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent \texttt{decide()} raises on a blank justification, and on a verdict its own rule does not give. A rule is a pure function from named values to the decision's outputs, versioned in its name, so a record naming \texttt{cax.\allowbreak{}summary/\allowbreak{}v1} names one function. Applied at the call and recorded with its inputs, it makes the decision replayable: anyone holding the code can re-derive the verdict from the record. The justification stays prose for a reader; the rule is what makes the record checkable against the code, which a sentence, written by a developer or a model~\cite{turpin2023}, is not. Lineage is declared, never inferred, except \texttt{wasInformedBy}, which PROV defines as derivable: inferring derivations from the order of calls produces confident, wrong edges. Durations come from a monotonic counter, because a wall clock that advances in 15.6 ms steps, as it does on our host, records most steps as taking no time.

\section{The Demonstrator}\label{sec:4}

\noindent A producer replays real yProv4ML records over a provenance bus (NATS) to four operational agents, which do work and emit typed PROV about themselves; two meta agents consume \emph{that} and emit PROV in turn. There is no agent framework: the agents are bare asyncio daemons, the strongest evidence that the library is not tied to one runtime. Fig.~\ref{fig:1} shows the architecture.

\noindent CA1 writes through the store's API, never the bus, which carries only a receipt (a \texttt{yp:\allowbreak{}pid} and a SHA-256). It persists \emph{after} consumption, at a quorum of consumer acknowledgements, with a timeout guard so a crashed consumer cannot lose a record; its \texttt{yp:\allowbreak{}escalation\_\allowbreak{}reason} separates three conditions demanding different responses: \emph{integrity}, \emph{incomplete-input} and \emph{consumption-unconfirmed}. Envelopes on the bus are numbered, and an agent that finds a gap records an \texttt{INCOMPLETE} decision rather than a well-formed record of a truncated input.

\noindent \textbf{Reflexivity is one level deep, by construction.} Meta agents publish to a subject prefix nothing subscribes to, so governance cannot re-trigger itself. Within that bound the meta agents are fully instrumented, and MC1 satisfies the standard it enforces. One container image serves all roles; CI runs both the containerised NATS path and an in-process path and holds each to the same lineage comparison.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:5}

\noindent The reference run is the two records bundled with yProv4ML through six agents: \textbf{24 PROV documents}, 96 activities and 228 structural relations. The corpus run adds 24 records trained for this paper. Every number is regenerated by code from an archive frozen from a clean commit with a hash of every file, named in the text by its directory under \texttt{experiments/\allowbreak{}}; 277 tests run on Linux and Windows. Code, vocabulary, corpus and archives are public (github.com/munyaradzi-zhou/yprov4agents-demonstrator) and will be deposited with a DOI.

\subsection{Conformance}\label{sec:5.1}

\noindent All 24 documents are accepted by the reference \texttt{prov} parser, and all 24 conform to the published shapes under \texttt{pyshacl}~\cite{pyshacl}, an external judge of an external rule. Of the 324 relations the records carry, 96 are one-per-activity attributions that say nothing about structure; the other 228 are 126 observed, 72 derived and 30 declared. Applied to its input, CA3 found real gaps in yProv4ML's records: 8 of 9 activities declare a start and no end.

\subsection{Capture, not retrieve}\label{sec:5.2}

\noindent We compare two records of the \emph{same run}. The callback baseline (A) is a handler we built, fed every step event a framework handler receives; it is an upper bound, because its events carry the structural facts the explicit instrumentation declared. Explicit capture (B) is our record. Fig.~\ref{fig:2} compares the two over the 24 records.

\noindent Structure is recoverable, and so are the verdicts, scraped from node labels, which a graph naming its decision node \texttt{decide} would defeat. No justification appears verbatim in the 12,425 characters A observed, but a run-time sentence rarely survives verbatim, so we ask instead about the values each decision's rule read, which the record names (Table~\ref{tab:3}).

\noindent A saw 2 of the 36: the wall clock span of each input run, a field of the yProv4ML record that crossed the boundary as part of $x$. Every computed value (gap statistics, byte counts, failed checks, ungrounded figures) was invisible, as Proposition 1 predicts. A looser test, extracting facts from the justification prose, finds 30 of the operational agents' 49 facts (61\%) visible, and the gap between the measures is the point: every fact in CA2's prose is visible to A, and neither value its rule read is. \textbf{The inputs to a decision may be observable; the values it computed, and the rule that combined them, are not.}

\noindent \textbf{At scale.} Two records cannot carry a claim about records in general, so we trained 24 small models for real and let yProv4ML record each run (\texttt{corpus/\allowbreak{}}, \texttt{experiments/\allowbreak{}corpus-24}): 288 decisions, all conforming to the shapes. A saw 24 of the 432 values the rules read (5.6\%), each again an input run's wall clock, while the prose-fact measure rose to 75\%: the more the input carries, the more of the prose an observer can match, and the less that says about the decision. The corpus also found what two records could not: CAx read the digits inside run names as asserted figures and said REVISE on half the runs, and CA3 found a 1.0 s stall before the first epoch of every run, 74 to 87\% of its wall clock. The first was a defect, now fixed; the second is a finding about the producer.

\noindent \textbf{A real framework.} A handler we wrote might see less than a real one, so we ran the four operational agents unmodified as nodes of a LangGraph graph~\cite{langgraph} over the same corpus, observed by a subclass of LangChain's own callback handler (\texttt{experiments/\allowbreak{}framework-baseline}). The framework hands its handler the whole graph state at every node: 45 times the bytes our handler saw. Of the 240 values the rules read, 110 match somewhere in it, and 106 of those match just as well in an unrelated run's observations: zeros, small counts, and two labels the agents print in their one-line results (CA1's trigger, CA2's chart subject). Four match only in their own run. A real framework shows its observer more of the input, and no more of the decision.

\noindent \textbf{Replay.} All 24 decisions replay, as do all 288 on the corpus and the nine of the fault scenarios, which between them reach every verdict and each of CA1's escalation reasons: the named rule, applied to the recorded inputs, gives the recorded outputs. Editing a stored verdict or input makes replay fail. The prose is the weaker record: it states 10 of the 36 values its rules read, and paraphrases the rest (``all supported'' for zero ungrounded figures) or omits them (a chart's byte count).

\noindent \textbf{Inside the step.} A line tracer is the observer Proposition 1 leaves out, so we built one: \texttt{sys.\allowbreak{}settrace} over the agents' own frames, keeping each change to a scalar local, on the same agents and producer record as the overhead study (\texttt{experiments/\allowbreak{}line-tracer}). It holds 7 of the 12 values the six rules read: CA3's five gap statistics, CA2's byte count and CA1's trigger. The other five (counts of failed checks, ungrounded figures and missing envelopes) were computed inside an expression and never assigned, so no tracer of locals sees them. The seven are among 15 to 116 distinct values per step, with nothing to mark them as the decision's inputs or to say what was done with them, and tracing multiplies the step's time by 2 to 13 where building our record is inside the noise. Seeing inside the step yields values, not the rule, and costs more than recording the rule.

\noindent The cost is visible in one agent. CA1 says ESCALATE for three reasons; A recovers the verdict, never the reason, so it cannot tell \emph{``storage is corrupting records''} from \emph{``a consumer is down''}.

\subsection{Model provenance}\label{sec:5.4}

\noindent Every model-bearing step records the model, sampling parameters, prompt identity, version and hash, token counts, latency and a response hash (\texttt{experiments/\allowbreak{}audit-llm}: four calls, 2,071 prompt and 480 completion tokens). The model a caller names is not the one that answers: the record carries the alias \texttt{gpt-4o}, the snapshot \texttt{gpt-4o-2024-08-06} and the provider's fingerprint, and the four calls of one run returned three fingerprints.

\subsection{Overhead}\label{sec:5.5}

\noindent Each agent's step was timed in 100 interleaved rounds under four conditions: provenance off, the callback baseline (A), built but not written (B-TRANS), and built and written (B) (\texttt{experiments/\allowbreak{}overhead-samples}). Failing to resolve a difference is not evidence of none, so we test equivalence: two one-sided tests at 5\% against a 1 ms margin, 0.06\% of the fastest model call in our run. \textbf{Building the record is equivalent to no provenance} for five of the six agents, at 48 to 81 \textmu{}s; CA2's 95 ms chart render jitters by more than the margin (interval $-$1.5 to +1.4 ms), so for it equivalence can be shown neither way. \textbf{Writing the record is the cost}: built and written, a record adds 0.75 to 1.3 ms, and the write dominates every resolved overhead (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}): MC1's 79 \textmu{}s step takes 1.05 ms with provenance, +1,232\%, 94\% of it the write. Beside a model call of 1.7 to 4.3 s, that write is under 0.1\%. Transient mode removes 89\% of the time cost; metadata mode 38\% of the bytes, keeping every justification. Justifications are 6.4\% of the records' bytes and a decision's rule and inputs 1.7\%, and cost is linear (fitted exponents 1.09 and 1.13 to 10,000 activities).

\subsection{Verdict reachability}\label{sec:5.6}

\noindent The ordinary run reaches six of twelve two-valued verdicts; nine injected scenarios reach the rest, each of CA1's three escalations, and \texttt{INCOMPLETE}: all thirteen are observed. Three scenarios are not faults: CA2 says REVISE on the bundled data because yProv4ML records carry no numeric values, a finding about the input. \texttt{mc1:\allowbreak{}FAIL} is unreachable from this library's own records, because \texttt{decide()} refuses a blank justification; it is reached by a third-party record, which is what a compliance checker is for.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:6}

\noindent \textbf{We show that justifications exist and that the decision rule is unrecoverable, not that they are good}; whether they improve an operator's decisions needs a human study. The fact-level measure is coarse in both directions. A model-bearing step is repeatable from its record, not reproducible: three fingerprints in one run is a backend changing under a caller that changed nothing. The bus is at-most-once: numbered envelopes expose a partial loss, but a stream lost whole is silent. The evaluation uses one producer (24 real records and two bundled ones) and one machine. We built the main baseline ourselves, granted it every declared structural fact, and checked it against a real framework's handler, which saw more bytes and no more of the decisions.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:7}

\noindent Provenance can describe what an agentic system did; with automatic capture alone it cannot describe why, because the rule behind a decision never leaves the agent. We made that measurable against a generous baseline, a line tracer and a real framework's handler; discharged it with a vocabulary and an API whose one hand-written call records a decision that replays from its own record; and found the cost to be the write, in time and in energy, while building the record is equivalent to not: \emph{storage for explainability, not speed for explainability}. Next: an OpenTelemetry bridge for the automatic half, bounded second-level reflexivity, meta agents that act on their verdicts, and a study of whether justifications improve operators' decisions.
